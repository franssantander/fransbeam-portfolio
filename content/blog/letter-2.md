> “If you suffer pain because of some external cause, what troubles you is not the thing but your decision about it.”
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 8.47

Marcus Aurelius had a simple habit whenever something began to look more impressive, frightening, or desirable than it really was. He would strip away the story surrounding it and describe it in the simplest terms possible. The goal was not to become cold or cynical, but to see things clearly before emotion had the chance to distort them.

Fine wine became fermented grape juice. Expensive purple robes became sheep’s wool dyed with shellfish. A beautiful piece of meat became a dead animal prepared for dinner. By removing the glamour around these things, Marcus reminded himself that appearances often have more power over us than the things themselves.

This is one of the quiet lessons of Stoicism: **learn to see things as they are, not only as they appear in your mind.**

We struggle with this more often than we realize. Someone cancels a meeting, and within seconds we tell ourselves that they do not respect our time. Business slows down for one month, and suddenly we imagine everything falling apart. Someone we like takes hours to reply, and our mind begins convincing us that they are losing interest.

The same thing happens at work. Someone criticizes something we created, and instead of simply hearing the criticism, we begin imagining what everyone must think of us. Maybe they think we are incompetent. Maybe they are disappointed. Maybe we are going to lose our job.

But none of those things necessarily happened.

**That is the problem.** The mind is quick to turn an event into a story. Something happens, and almost immediately we attach meaning to it. Before we even understand what is going on, we are already reacting to an interpretation rather than reality itself.

The Stoics called our first reaction to something an *impression*. Fear may appear. Anger may rise. Embarrassment, sadness, jealousy, or desire may arrive before we have time to reason with them. We cannot always prevent that first emotional response.

But what happens after that is where our responsibility begins.

We can question the impression.

We can ask whether what we are thinking is actually true.

We can choose to see the situation more clearly.

## 1. Describe, Don't Judge

When something bothers you, try removing the emotional language from the situation. Adjectives can quietly turn facts into judgments, and judgments can quickly become stories.

Instead of saying:

*He rudely ignored my message.*

Say:

*I sent him a message yesterday. He hasn’t replied.*

The first sentence already assumes intention. The second simply describes what happened.

Instead of:

*The meeting was a complete disaster.*

Try:

*The investors disagreed with my proposal, and we did not reach a decision.*

One creates drama. The other gives you something you can actually work with. When you remove unnecessary judgment from an event, you give yourself room to think. You stop fighting the version of reality created by your emotions and begin dealing with what is actually in front of you.

## 2. Don’t Turn Fear Into a Prediction

When something difficult happens, separate the event from what you are making it mean.

Take a piece of paper and divide it into two sides.

On one side, write **what happened.**

On the other, write **what you think it means.**

For example:

*My client ended our contract.*

That is the fact.

Then your mind might say:

*I’m losing momentum. More clients will leave. My business might fail.*

Those thoughts may feel real, but feeling real does not make them facts.

Some of your fears may eventually happen. Most may never happen at all. But until you have evidence, you are suffering over a future that exists only in your imagination.

Philosophy asks you to bring those thoughts into the light and examine them calmly.

What do you actually know?

What are you assuming?

What are you afraid might happen?

There is a big difference between those three things.

## 3. Decide What Comes Next

Once you understand the facts, stop feeding the story and ask a better question:

**What can I do next?**

Your client ended the contract.

Okay.

Do you need to understand why? Do you need to replace the lost revenue? Is there something about the product that needs improvement? Should you review your sales pipeline? Or do you simply need to accept that the relationship has reached its end?

Reality gives you something to respond to. Imagination often gives you something to suffer over. This is why clarity matters. When you stop exaggerating what happened, you can finally decide what deserves your attention. You become less concerned with the story in your head and more concerned with the action available to you now. And this practice applies almost everywhere.

See the situation clearly.

Deal with what is there.

And leave the rest of the story unwritten.
